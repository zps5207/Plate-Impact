Answer. Most surviving ghosts are not a mismatch between the VU1 and the ghost. For 74 of the 85 surviving ghosts within 10 mm of the axis, the VU1 on the same nodes has not met its failure criterion either. They are intact because the ball passed through them without loading them.

The ball kept 983 of its 1000 m/s and the plate absorbed about 1% of its energy. At the peak, 490 ball nodes were inside live ghost elements at once, and 643 different live ghosts were entered by a ball node at some time. Contact pressure showed up on only 285 of 27,401 plate nodes, usually for a single output frame.

What was run

Three passes over the 6 GB ODB, every frame, with numpy bulk reads:

  1. Inventory: fields, history, instances, sets, energies.
  2. VU1 reconstruction for all 24,000 hosts. The ODB has no VU1 state, so I rebuilt the VU1 failure test from node displacements, using the same 8-Gauss-point Green-Lagrange normal-strain measure and detF test as VUEL_failure.for (EPS_FAIL 0.1138, JAC_FAIL 0.05). I compared each host's predicted failure time with its ghost's deletion time, PEEQ and Mises.
  3. Contact and penetration: CPRESS and CSHEARMAG footprint, ball nodes inside live ghost bounding boxes (shrunk 10%, every 5th frame), and the ball's closest approach to every ghost within 10 mm.

Check on the reconstruction. It predicts 1364 VU1 failures. The solver log printed 1364 VUEL failure messages. The counts match exactly, so the reconstruction is a good stand-in for the hidden VU1 state. All come from the strain test; none from the detF test.

2. The ball passes through

  • Every one of the 58 islands was entered by a ball node while alive (58 of 58). The ball surface came within 0.5 mm of all 85 surviving near-axis ghosts, median 0.16 mm.
  • 103 of the 643 ghosts that a ball node entered are still alive at the end, 89 of them with the VU1 not failed.
  • 285 plate nodes ever had CPRESS above zero, mostly for one output frame. Median peak pressure on them is 4.0e+10 Pa, maximum 6.1e+11 Pa: impulsive spikes, not sustained contact.
  • Energy: kinetic 4094 to 4015, internal 27, plastic dissipation 24.5, viscous 11, friction 2. About 1% of the ball's energy went into the plate.

What this says

  1. Verified: the contact does not stop the ball. It acts on a few nodes at a time while whole elements sit inside the ball, including all 58 islands.
  2. Verified: most survivors have an intact VU1 as well (74 of 85 near the axis), so there is no failed VU1 for the ghost to follow. A true VU1-failed, ghost-alive mismatch affects 104 hosts in the whole plate.
  3. Hypothesis, not tested: the contact is weak because the contact surface belongs to the ghost elements, whose stiffness and density are scaled by 1e-4. Abaqus derives penalty contact stiffness from the surface elements. The VU1 mass and stiffness sit on the same nodes but are not part of the surface.
  4. Other candidates: the element-based surface holds only the original outer faces, so faces exposed by deletion never join it; or a steel ball at 1000 m/s against a 5 MPa matrix is a stiffness mismatch the default penalty cannot resolve.
  5. Caveat on the opening claim: the island ghosts' Mises stress was still rising at the final frame, so a few may simply need more time. I expect little change, since the ball is far below the plate by then.

Experiments I would run next

None have been run. Each changes the deck, so I would only submit with your go-ahead.

  1. Contact stiffness. Same deck with the ghost scaled to 1e-2 instead of 1e-4. If penetration falls, the hypothesis holds. This changes the ghost, so it is your decision.
  2. Contact definition. ALL EXTERIOR with a penalty stiffness scale factor on the general contact, or node-based surfaces on the plate nodes.
  3. Longer step to 1.2e-4 s, to check whether the loading island ghosts eventually yield.
  4. A one-hex test deck (one plate element and a small impactor) to learn the penalty stiffness quickly without the 33 minute run.
